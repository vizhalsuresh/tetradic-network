# Sanzo Wada (和田 三造) — Haishoku Sōkan (配色総鑑, 1933)
## Historical Context, Aesthetic Philosophy, and Translation to Creative Coding

---

## 1. The Author and the Historical Moment

**Sanzo Wada (1883–1967)** was a monumental figure in 20th-century Japanese art, visual design, and color science. 

* **The Renaissance Figure:** Trained in both traditional Japanese painting (*Nihonga*) and Western oil painting (*Yōga*), Wada was also a master printmaker, textile designer, and costume director. In 1954, he won the **Academy Award for Best Costume Design** for the legendary color film *Gate of Hell* (*Jigokumon*, directed by Teinosuke Kinugasa), widely celebrated as one of the most visually stunning color films in cinema history.
* **The 1927 Mission:** In 1927, seeing that rapid industrialization was eroding traditional Japanese craftsmanship and flooding modern cities with haphazard, discordant synthetic colors, Wada established the **Japan Standard Color Association** (日本標準色協会, now the *Japan Color Research Institute* / 日本色彩研究所).
* **The Landmark Work (1933–1935):** Between 1933 and 1935, Wada published the 6-volume masterwork **『配色総鑑』 (*Haishoku Sōkan*)** through Hakubisha. This was the first systematic color combination treatise ever produced in Japan, featuring 348 original multi-color plates across duos, trios, and quads.

---

## 2. The Science: Why the Colors are Named This Way

A common mystery for people encountering Wada's book is the curious nature of the English color names (e.g., *Hermosa Pink*, *Corinthian Pink*, *Cameo Pink*, *Hay's Russet*, *Calamine Blue*, *Cotinga Purple*).

* **Robert Ridgway's 1912 Scientific Standard:** Wada adopted the nomenclature of American ornithologist and Smithsonian curator **Robert Ridgway**, whose 1912 treatise *Color Standards and Color Nomenclature* was the global benchmark for biological and mineralogical taxonomy.
* **The Western-Eastern Synthesis:** Wada was deliberately bridging worlds. He took Ridgway's scientific ornithological taxonomy (named after bird plumage, tropical flora, and rare minerals) and mapped it directly onto traditional Japanese botanical dyes (*Nihon no Dentōshoku*—e.g., madder, indigo, persimmon tannin, gardenia, iris, sumi ink).
* **The Result:** The palettes are not synthetic mathematical divisions of an RGB color wheel; they are **biomorphic and mineral harmonies** rooted in millions of years of evolutionary nature and centuries of Japanese artisanal culture.

## 3. Wada's Core Aesthetic Principles

### A. Tonal Tension Over Sterile Match (Asymmetrical Harmony)
Modern digital palettes generated by algorithms usually rely on clean geometric spacing (exact 180° complements or clean 120° triadic wheels). Wada deliberately avoided this. He paired colors with subtle tonal dissonance—such as pairing an earthy, muted vegetable green with an opulent dusty rose, or deep Prussian slate with unbleached ochre. This tension creates **visual friction without harshness**.

### B. The Four Seasonal Motifs (*Kisetsu*)
In Japanese aesthetics, color is fundamentally dynamic and seasonal:
1. **Spring (*Haru*):** High luminance, delicate pastels, mineral pinks, and tender bamboo shoots. Evokes birth, awakening, and gentle optimism.
2. **Summer (*Natsu*):** Deep saturated indigos, cerulean streams, verdant moss, and cooling slates. Evokes vitality, freshness, and focused energy.
3. **Autumn (*Aki*):** Russets, persimmon, dried rush, amber, smoked tea, and rich ochre. Evokes harvest, wisdom, grounded maturity, and warmth.
4. **Winter (*Fuyu*):** Sumi ink, slate gray, pine needles, cold plum, and bone white. Evokes stillness, austere dignity, and deep contemplation.

### C. The Aesthetic Polarity: *Shibui* vs. *Modan*
* **Shibui / Iki (渋い / 粋):** Subdued, understated, mature beauty where nothing screams for attention. Low saturation, nuanced middle tones, and quiet authority.
* **Modan (モダン - 1930s Showa Modernism):** Bold, cosmopolitan contrast reflecting Art Deco architecture, jazz-age Tokyo, and early industrial machinery.

## 4. Translation to YouTube Coded Content Generation

In algorithmic art, creative coding (p5.js, Three.js, Canvas), and programmatic video generation (Remotion, Manim, Motion Canvas), **color is the single highest leverage point for channel identity and viewer retention**.

### The Problem with Most Coded YouTube Channels
Most programmatic video generators fall into one of two traps:
1. **The Neon Cyberpunk Trap:** Over-saturated cyan (#00FFFF) and magenta (#FF00FF) against pure black (#000000). While striking for 5 seconds, this causes severe viewer eye fatigue and looks like a generic amateur tutorial.
2. **The Monochromatic Terminal Trap:** Default dark gray with pure white text and lime green highlights. Clean, but completely forgettable.

### How Wada's System Transforms Programmatic Video
1. **Organic Elegance at 60 FPS:** Because Wada's colors are rooted in mineral and biological pigments, complex particle systems, geometric fractals, and mathematical curves render with the quality of museum prints or high-budget documentary film.
2. **Semantic Role Architecture:**
   * **Canvas (60%):** Never pure #000000 or #FFFFFF. Use Wada's deep slates (#12354e, #253122) or warm unbleached parchment (#ffefae, #efece6) to ground the scene.
   * **Primary Geometry / Typography / Code (30%):** High-contrast harmonic pair (minimum 4.5:1 WCAG ratio) ensuring mathematical formulas and code syntax remain razor sharp on mobile screens.
   * **Kinetic Particles / Accents (10%):** The vibrant swatch in the combination reserved for cursor glows, particle bursts, active nodes, and key data points.
3. **Color Scripting Across Video Chapters:**
   In a 10-minute coded video, switching combinations per chapter (e.g., moving from an Autumn palette for "The Problem" to a Summer palette for "The Solution") guides viewer psychology without requiring explicit visual cues.
